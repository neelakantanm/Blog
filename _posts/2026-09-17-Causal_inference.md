I love reading about statistical paradoxes. The usual classics like [Berkskon's pardox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berkson%27s_paradox), [Simpson's paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simpson%27s_paradox) and [Monty Hall problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monty_Hall_problem) are part of my all-time favorites. For a while, I had been trying to learn more about such statistical paradoxes, and I found a whole list of such ostensible "paradoxes" that occur in statistical analysis. Here is a list of some of the major ones: [statistical paradoxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category:Statistical_paradoxes). The conundrum I had was that most of the time I understood the solution to these riddles once the statement of the paradox was shown to me, or in the worst-case scenario I could quickly verify the solution once it was shown to me, however, I didn't have a clue as to why such paradoxes existed, or when one can expect to encounter such a fault in our intuition when doing statistical analysis of raw data. This eventually led me to the book *The Book of Why* by Judea Pearl.

It was truly an enlightening experience learning about the "causal revolution" as Pearl puts it. Let me start by stating the three rungs of causal inference as defined by Pearl in the book: Rung 1,2 and 3 are "association", "doing" and "imagination" respectively. You might have heard about the common phrase in statistics, "correlation does not imply causation". This difference between correlation and causality is what that the three rungs discerns. 

* The first rung is about correlation, for example computing $$P(X\mid Y), P(Y\mid X)$$ and $$ P(X, Y)$$ (joint distribution) for two random variables $$X, Y$$. Observe that computing these probabilites don't require you understand if $$X$$ is cause of $$Y$$ (vice versa) or not. For example, say $$X$$ is the random variable "smoking", and "Y" the random variable "lung cancer" (by abuse of notation we will also use variables $$X, Y$$ to denote the events smoking=True, lung cancer= True respectively). So, we can ask questions like "how correlated are smaking and cancer?", "what is the probability that you have lung cancer given that you have been smoking", "what is the probability that you have been smking given that you have lung cancer?" etc. 

* The second rung is more than just correlations. Continuing the previous example, in the second rung,  we want to ask questions like "what is the probability that you have lung cancer given that you have been forced to smoke?". Observe that this questions cannot be answered by just finding conditional probabilties $$P(X\mid Y)$$ and $$P(Y\mid X)$$. It was a big debate smong statisticians and doctors in the mid-twentieth century whether "smoking causes cancer or not". Reknowned statistician Ronald Fisher was a proponent of the idea that there might be a smoking gene in humans that makes someone want to smoke [^gene]. So, it could be that the smoking gene causes cancer, and that smoking is just a mediator between smoking genge and lung cancer. Therefore, one cannot claim that $$P(X\mid Y)$$ is same as $$P(X \mid \text{do smoking})$$. This is one way to see why we cannot simply answer the questions in rung 2 just by looking at the distribution of $$X$$ and $$Y$$; one needs to have a world model that relates the two variables. In other words, it is important to know how the data was collected to answer questions in rung 2. Pearl introduces a $$do$$ operator to denote the doing action (see [do calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Do-calculus)), i.e., $$\textrm{do}(X)$$ denotes the operation "do smoking". So, we are interested in the probabilties like $$P(X\mid \textrm{do}(Y)), P(Y\mid \textrm{do}(X))$$ in rung 2. Observe that it is not ethical to force someone to smoke for a decade or so to collect data to see if smoking actually causes cancer. This was one of the major reasons why statisticians and doctors were stuck in a limbo as neither could the statistics of the data help nor could the docotors collect the relevant data to resolve the issue [^causalungcancer].

* The third rung is highest rung, the rung of imagination or the rung of counterfactuals. In this rung, we want to ask questions like "suppose Bob has been smoking and has lung cancer, what is the the probability that Bob has lung cancer given that Bob didn't smoke for the past thirty years?". So, you are imagining a world that is different from what is factually true. i.e, we want to compute $$P(Y_{\neg X}\mid X,Y)$$, where $$Y_{\neg X}$$ denotes the event "has cancer and didn't smoke". The standard theory of probability is not equipped to handle such situations, and asking to compute $$P(Y_{\neg X}\mid X,Y)$$ might even seem ridiculuous to someone who is formally trained in porbability theory. However, humans compute and imagine such situations in our mind all the time. Again, the probabilites $$P(X\mid Y), P(X\mid \textrm{do}(Y)), \cdots$$ is not sufficient to answer such questions. We won't get into the intricacies of solving such counterfactual questions here though; an interested reader can read about it in chapter 8 of Pearl's book.

[^gene]: Fisher was right to some extent. There is indeed a gene that increases smkking tendency: https://www.newswise.com/articles/genetic-connection-to-cigarette-smoking
[^causalungcancer]: See Chapter 5 of Pearl's book to learn about the resolution of this issue using causal diagrams.
