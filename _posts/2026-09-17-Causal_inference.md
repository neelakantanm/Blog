I love reading about statistical paradoxes! The usual classics like [Berkskon's pardox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berkson%27s_paradox), [Simpson's paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simpson%27s_paradox) and [Monty Hall problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monty_Hall_problem) are part of my all-time favorites. For a while, I have been trying to learn more about such statistical paradoxes, and I found a whole list of such apparent "paradoxes" coming in statistical analysis. Here is a list of some of the major ones: [statistical paradoxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category:Statistical_paradoxes). Most of the time, I understood the solution to these riddles once the statement of the paradox was shown to me, or in the worst-case scenario I could quikcly verify the solution once it was shown to me. However, I didn't have clue as to why such paradoxes existed, or when one can expect to encounter such a fault in our intuition when doing statistical analysis of raw data. This eventually led me to the book *The Book of Why* by Judea Pearl.

It was a truly enlightening experience learning about the "causal revolution" as Pearl puts it.
