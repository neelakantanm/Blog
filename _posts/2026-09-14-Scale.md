*Scale* by [Geoffrey West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_West) is one of the books that I recently read, which gave me an intellectual epiphany as it preoccupied my mind with various interesting ideas that I was able to turn into thought experiments[^thought], neural network experiments, data analysis of biological experiments, and more excitingly, reconceptualization of some of my own mathematical work [^math_scaling]. If you want to read about my thesis on [neural scaling laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neural_scaling_law), you can skip to the section [Neural scaling laws](#neural-scaling-laws).

Let me begin with a brief overview of the book. When West reached his fifties, he began reflecting deeply on aging and the finite lifespan of human beings as he was afraid that he might die soon since his family had a history of short-lived males. So, he pivoted his research from theoretical physics to biology in order give an explanation for different scaling laws we observe in nature, and thereby, potentially give a satisfactory answer to the maximum observed lifespan of human beings. The book goes through a wide variety of topics through the lens of scaling: Why do organisms and ecosystems ranging from cells and whales to forests scale with size in a remarkably universal, systematic, and predictable fashion? Why do we stop growing? Why do we get relatively far fewer tumors than mice, while whales get almost none? Why do almost all companies live for only a relatively few years whereas cities keep growing and manage to circumvent the apparently inevitable fate that befalls even the most powerful and seemingly invulnerable companies? How do we ensure that our human-engineered systems, which evolved only over the past ten thousand years, can continue to coexist with the natural biological world, which evoled over billions of years? 
 
I won't get into the intricacies of scaling laws for cities and companies in this blog as I don't want the blog to be long. However, I will provide examples of a few other interesting scaling laws expounded in the book. As a warm-up, here is a scaling law that we all encounter in our daily life: The [square-cube law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square–cube_law). i.e., the "strength" of an object (say cube shaped) scales as a square of the sidelength and the volume of the object scales as a cube of the sidelength. So, if we make the object scale isometrically in length, the object will crumble under its own weight due to the gravity as the the cubic function (its weight) grows faster and will eventually be too much for the square function (the strength) to handle. This is one of the reason why it is scientifically impossible to isometrically scale humans or animals as we see in the movies; West talks about mathematical impossibility of the existence of Godzilla in the book.

## Kleiber's law and fractals
[Kleiber's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kleiber%27s_law) states the graph of logarithm of body mass (kg) of mammals vs metabolic rates (watts) is roughly is a straight line with slope $$3/4$$. Following is the original graph drawn by Kleiber in early 1930s after analyzing animals ranging from a small dove weighing 150 grams to a large steer weighing 1000 kg.  
<img src="{{ '/assets/css/images/kleiber.jpg' | relative_url }}" style="display: block; width: 320px; max-width: 100%; height: auto; margin: 1rem auto 0;">
In fact, over the years researchers have verified this scaling law entire spectrum of mammals ranging from smalles, the shrew, to the larges, the blue whale, thereby covering more than eight orders of magniture in mass. In fact, a scaling law corresponding to variations of $$\pm 1/4$$ modulo $$0$$ or $$1$$ is universal in nature. To quote from the book *Scale* : "the expoenent for growth rates is close to $$3/4$$, for lenghts of aortas and genomes it's $$1/4$$, for heights of trees $$1/4$$, for cross-sectional areas of both aortas and tree trunks $$3/4$$, for brain sizes $$3/4$$, for cerebral white and gray matters $$5/4$$, for heart rates $$-1/4$$, for mitochondrial densities in cells $$-1/4$$, for rates of evolution $$-1/4$$, ... and many, many more."

When I learned about these scaling laws while reading *Scale*, I couldn't believe what I was seeing. How could such such a simple scaling law, which are variations of $$1/4$$ work universally in nature? Being a harmonic analyst I have seen such a phenomenon in many theorems and inequalities in various fields of harmonic analysis: for example variations of $$\frac{2(n+1)}{n-1}, \frac{2n}{n-1}$$ appear as critical exponents in [restriction theory](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2020/03/29/247b-notes-1-restriction-theory/), which has to do with the fractal dimension of "critical objects" involed (I am being a little vague here). It turns out that the quarter scaling law can be explained using fractal dimensions, which we will see next.

### An explanation: effective fractal dimension
In the seminal paper [West, Brown and Enquist 1997 Science paper](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.276.5309.122?utm_source=chatgpt.com), gave an argument for why the quarter scaling laws show up universally. They argue that the surfaces responsible for metabolism are not ordinary external surfaces: they include capillaries, lungs, leaves, roots and mitochondrial inner membranes. These exchange surfaces are folded and distributed throughout the organism. Because they reach nearly every part of the body, these surfaces are space filling and hence have an effective dimension (not to be confused with [effective Hausdorff dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effective_dimension)) close to $$3$$. 
This does not mean that an individual capillary or mitochondrial crista has dimension 3 [^box-dim_cristae]. It describes how the total exchange area of the network scales as the biological system becomes larger. 

Suppose you consider an orgranism which is scaled ([allometrically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allometry)) by a factor of $$\lambda$$ relative to an average human. Now, we want to transport say oxygen, blood, etc for a distance $$\ell$$ through this organism. Natural selection is assumed to keep transport paths relatively direct and efficient, so this distance retains ordinary dimension 1:

$$
\ell\propto\lambda.
$$

The biologically active volume ($$v$$) is represented approximately as exchange area ($$a$$) multiplied by transport distance:
$$ v\sim a\ell. $$ From the effective dimension of exchange surfaces, we see that exchange area grows as $$\lambda^3$$.   Consequently,

$$ v\propto\lambda^3\cdot \lambda=\lambda^4. $$

 Hence, the biological volume grows as $$\lambda^4$$. Therefore,

$$ a\propto v^{3/4}. $$

If biological volume is proportional to body mass ($$B$$) and metabolic rate ($$M$$) is proportional to exchange area[^metabolism_surface_area], then

$$M\propto B^{3/4},$$

which explains the Kleiber's law. 

## Neural scaling laws
Neural scaling law is a statistical law that predicts the peformance of a [deeping learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_learning) model when the the number of parameters (i.e., the size of the model) and amount of data is scaled. Of course, the exact constants in the law depends on the specific model. In other words, the law predicts the cost to performace ratio of a given model. Two of the seminal papers that helped fuel the race among leading AI companies to use ever-greater amounts of compute and train increasingly large language models are [Kaplan et al, 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.08361) and [Hoffmann et al, 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.15556). To give you gist of the scaling laws, here are three graphs from Kaplan et al. 

<img src="{{ '/assets/css/images/kaplan-1.png' | relative_url }}" style="display: block; width: 320 px; max-width: 100%; height: auto; margin: 1rem auto 0;">

i.e, the performance of a neural language model increases according to a power law with increase in amount of data and size of the model. It is still a mystery why the neural scaling laws behave the way they do; see [Sharma, Kaplan, 2022](https://jmlr.org/papers/v23/20-1111.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [Bahri, Kaplan and Sharma, 2024](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2311878121?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [Havrilla, Lia, 2024](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/4a68bb0e075a3215d4ff37382ea598dc-Abstract-Conference.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com) to see some explanations based on the assumption that the data is concentrated near a low dimensional manifold. 

Having seen the Kleiber's law based on fractal dimensions, I was curious to see if such a fractal dimensional explanation could exist for neural scaling laws. I was intigued to learn that certain statistical representations of natural language exhibit [multifractal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multifractal_system) behaviour, reflecting heterogeneous correlations across multiple textual scales, for example, see [Grabska-Gradzińska et al, 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1212.3171). Having learned about this, I decided to do some expriments by myself to see if neural scaling laws in certain simple cases can be explained using fractal dimension (a simple case of multi-fractal spectrum). There are some results in the literature along these directions, but we are far from giving any satisfactory explanations. 

*Thesis: Neural scaling laws (at least qualitatively) for language models emerge from multi-fractal spectrum of natural language.*

Let me give you a heuristic explanation. Suppose the data we have is supported on a fractal set $$S$$ of dimension $$d$$ (any notion of fractal dimension will do), and the neural netwrork we are training the data on is a ReLU-feedforward-multilayer-percetron ([MLP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multilayer_perceptron) for short) to approximate a function defined on $$S$$. The important geometric fact is that resolving a $$d$$-dimensional fractal down to scale $$r$$ requires roughly 
$$
\sim r^{-d}
$$
cells. Thus higher-dimensional fractal supports contain more distinguishable regions at a given resolution. 

Suppose further that the target function is $$\beta$$-[Hölder continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hölder_condition). Then, approximating it only down to spatial resolution $$r$$ produces an error of order $$r^\beta$$, or roughly $$r^{2\beta}$$ under squared loss. If a neural network with $$N$$ effective degrees of freedom can resolve on the order of $$N$$ such cells, then

$$
N\sim r^{-d},
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
r\sim N^{-1/d}.
$$

Substituting this into the approximation error gives the heuristic scaling law

$$
\text{Loss as function of }\ N\sim N^{-2\beta/d}.
$$

Thus, at least qualitatively, increasing the fractal dimension makes the learning problem harder and produces a smaller scaling exponent: more parameters are required to resolve a higher-dimensional set to the same accuracy. I ran an [experiment](https://github.com/neelakantanm/Cantor-set-MLP--scaling-laws/blob/main/01_cantor_multiscale_dimension_scaling.ipynb) and observed that training on Cantor set as the data and a feedforward-ReLU-MLP as the neural network exhibits qualitatively slower parameter-scaling as the fractal dimension of the support increases, consistent with a covering-number explanation of neural scaling laws I just explained. I believe that the full potential of the ideas we explored here remains untapped, and can be pushed further.
